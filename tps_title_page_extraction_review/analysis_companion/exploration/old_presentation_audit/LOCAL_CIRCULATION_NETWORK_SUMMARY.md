# Experiment 4: Local Print Geography Network

Purpose: test the old claim about local roots and cross-European print reach without using incomplete author bibliographies as the main denominator.

Terminology note: this experiment does **not** reconstruct circulation in the full book-history sense. A book printed in one city could be sold, owned, read, or taught elsewhere. The data here supports claims about recorded print locations, reprint links, title-page language, and named author/editor lines. Therefore the safer historical vocabulary is **print geography**, **observed print reach**, **printing profile**, or **geographic print profile**, not circulation in general.

This experiment uses the metadata-defined Elements corpus, not all books by the same people. That matters: the corpus is complete for the recorded Elements editions and their reprints, but it is not a complete bibliography of every author/editor, and it is not a complete corpus of all early modern mathematical print.

## Main Historical Result

The Elements corpus is locally anchored but not simply local. Its observed print geography is built from strong city centers, mostly same-language reprint paths, and a smaller set of cross-city routes that place particular versions in recognizable regional corridors.

The old claim should therefore be revised:

> Instead of saying that authors or adapters were generally locally rooted, say that Elements editions show a mixed print-geographic pattern: heavy local reprinting in major centers, selective regional reprinting of specific versions, and limited but visible language transfer.

This is stronger than an author-career claim because it is grounded in the complete metadata-defined Elements edition corpus.

The most useful pattern is not "authors are local" or "authors are international." The Elements corpus contains several geographic print profiles.

### 1. Single-City Serial Anchors

Examples: Sacrobosco in Paris; Dasypodius in Strasbourg; Tartaglia in Venice.

This profile is geographically narrow. In the strongest recurring cases, the print record is renewed repeatedly in one print center rather than appearing across cities. In the many one-edition cases, it marks a named rendering that enters the corpus only once and does not generate a visible multi-city line in the current metadata.

- Sacrobosco: 9 editions, 1500-1538, all Paris, all Latin, all folio. This is the most materially monumental version of the single-city type: a Parisian Latin folio series.
- Dasypodius: 11 editions, 1557-1599, all Strasbourg, Greek/Latin, mostly octavo with some quarto. This is a school/humanist-looking single-city profile: one city, compact formats, bilingual learned identity.
- Tartaglia: 5 editions, 1543-1586, all Venice, Italian/Latin, folio/quarto/octavo. This is a Venetian workshop/commercial profile: one city, but with more format experimentation and one cross-language reprint signal in the current cluster data.

Historical claim: single-city does not mean marginal. These are local centers strong enough to keep a Euclidean line alive across decades. Format separates them: Sacrobosco is large-format Parisian authority; Dasypodius is compact Strasbourg pedagogy; Tartaglia is Venetian commercial/mathematical repackaging.

### 2. Regional Vernacular Consolidation

Examples: Dou in Dutch cities; Henrion in Paris/Rouen.

This profile moves within a vernacular region while remaining linguistically stable.

- Dou: 11 editions, 1606-1701, 4 cities, all Dutch, mostly octavo with some quarto. His spread is urban-regional: Amsterdam, Leiden, Rotterdam, Utrecht. The long span, single language, and compact formats point to durable vernacular use rather than occasional translation.
- Henrion: 11 editions, 1615-1685, Paris/Rouen/Rouen-Paris, all French, mostly octavo. He has 6 reprint links and 6 explicit translation cases, all tied to Latin-to-French transfer in the current metadata.

Historical claim: the vernacular Elements is not just a one-time act of access. In Dou and Henrion it becomes a local-language print product capable of reprinting, regional reappearance, and long-term reuse. Henrion is especially important because his French Elements is both vernacular and explicitly translational; Dou is more purely regional-vernacular.

### 3. Latin Institutional Mobility

Examples: Clavius, Tacquet; partly Magnien-Gracile.

This profile moves across cities while keeping Latin stable.

- Clavius: 7 editions, 1574-1654, 4 cities, Latin, formats 2/4/8. The city spread Rome, Cologne, Frankfurt, Mainz suggests institutional learned portability.
- Tacquet: 7 editions, 1654-1701, 4 cities, Latin, all octavo. The spread Amsterdam, Antwerp, Leuven, Padua shows a late seventeenth-century Latin school/institutional path in a compact format.
- Magnien-Gracile: 10 editions, 1557-1627, Paris/Cologne, Latin, all octavo, with 7 reprint links, including 5 cross-city links. This is the clearest reprint-network case: not broad city diversity, but a specific Paris-Cologne Latin route.

Historical claim: Latin allows the Elements to appear across cities without being culturally remade as a vernacular object. But this Latin print mobility has subtypes: Clavius is broad institutional prestige; Tacquet is compact late school print geography; Magnien-Gracile is a strong two-city reprint corridor.

### 4. Multi-Language Late-Seventeenth-Century Mediation

Examples: Dechales, Barrow, Fournier.

This profile combines city movement, language movement, and format flexibility. It is especially visible after 1640.

- Dechales: 16 editions, 1660-1703, 7 city labels, 3 languages, 5 formats, 3 translation cases. He is the most flexible profile in the table: French/Latin/English, Lyon/Paris/Amsterdam/Lausanne/Oxford/London, and formats from quarto to duodecimo and smaller.
- Barrow: 10 editions, 1655-1705, 3 cities, English/Latin, 4 formats, 3 reprint links. His profile is Anglo-Latin and university/print-centered: Cambridge/London plus Osnabrück.
- Fournier: 6 editions, 1643-1665, 3 cities, French/Latin, octavo/duodecimo. His profile is smaller but similar in kind: French/Latin portability across Paris, London, and Cambridge.

Historical claim: by the later seventeenth century, some Elements lines appear less as stable local products and more as adaptable pedagogical packages. Dechales is the strongest example: the same named source can be printed across languages, cities, and material formats. This is exactly where "mediation" becomes a geographic print profile, not only a title-page rhetoric.

### Profile Taxonomy

| Profile | Movement | Language | Format signal | Historical meaning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single-city serial anchor | Repeated editions in one city | Usually Latin or learned bilingual | Folio for authority; quarto/octavo for school/commercial uses | Local print depth |
| Regional vernacular consolidation | Movement within one vernacular region | One vernacular | Mostly octavo/quarto | Stable local-language Euclid |
| Latin institutional mobility | Several cities, Latin unchanged | Latin | Octavo often dominant; sometimes mixed with folio/quarto | Learned/institutional portability |
| Multi-language mediation | Cities plus language transfer | Latin/French/English etc. | Most format-flexible | Late-century pedagogical adaptation |

This makes the print-geography claim sharper: the Elements does not simply appear more locally or more widely. Its observed print reach takes different forms, and those forms combine place, language, format, and title-page mediation differently.

### Geographic Reach Of Same-Author Elements Lines

The next refinement asks whether same-author/editor renderings of the Elements are local, regional, routed between two regions, or pan-European/multi-region.

Generated by:

`scripts/classify_geographic_reach_and_correlates.py`

Main outputs:

- `experiment_outputs/local_circulation_network/to_1705_named_adapter_geographic_reach.csv`
- `experiment_outputs/local_circulation_network/to_1705_named_adapter_geographic_reach_counts.csv`

Definitions:

- single city = all editions in the named line are printed in one city;
- single region = editions appear in multiple cities but within one mapped region, for example France, Italy, Low Countries, German lands;
- interregional route = editions connect two regions through a limited route, usually two or three cities, for example Paris-Cologne, London-Paris, Basel-Venice;
- pan-European/multi-region = editions span more than a simple two-city/two-region route, either because they cover more than two regions or because they form a wider multi-city cross-regional network.

Author-line counts:

| Geographic reach | Named lines | Share of named lines | Editions | Share of editions |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Single city | 90 | 75.0% | 149 | 52.1% |
| Interregional route | 17 | 14.2% | 64 | 22.4% |
| Single region | 10 | 8.3% | 43 | 15.0% |
| Pan-European/multi-region | 3 | 2.5% | 30 | 10.5% |

Examples:

- single city: Sacrobosco in Paris; Dasypodius in Strasbourg; Tartaglia in Venice; Forcadel in Paris;
- single region: Dou in the Low Countries; Henrion in France; Le Mardelé in France; Commandino/Spacciuoli in Italy; Doino in Italy;
- interregional route: Magnien-Gracile between Paris and Cologne; Fournier between Paris and England; Jean de la Pène between Paris and Leiden; Zamberti between Venice and Basel;
- pan-European/multi-region: Dechales across France, England, the Low Countries, and Swiss/Upper Rhine; Clavius across Italy and German lands; Tacquet across the Low Countries and Italy.

This supports the broad claim that same-author Elements lines do not have one printing pattern, but it also changes the balance of the argument. Once one-off and two-edition named renderings are included, the largest group by far is single-city: 90/120 named lines, or 75.0%. Many named renderings of Euclid therefore do not become visible multi-city lines in the current corpus. At the same time, the smaller multi-city groups carry a disproportionate number of editions: pan-European/multi-region lines are only 3/120 named lines, but they account for 30/286 editions, or 10.5%.

### Candidate Correlates Of Geographic Reach

The tempting expectation would be that print reach maps neatly onto intellectual and social style: practical books stay local; vernacular books stay regional; Latin books appear more widely; books 1-6 behave differently from near-complete Euclid; institutional display predicts a school network; restoration, method, augmentation, or correction define distinctive routes.

The data does not support such a neat typology.

Edition-level matched-feature rates:

| Reach | Matched editions | Practical | Books 1-6/solids | Vernacular | Small format, 8mo+ | Institution | Jesuit | Pedagogy/access | Method/order | Augmentation | Restoration | Correction |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Single city | 132 | 8.3% | 33.3% | 51.5% | 43.9% | 38.6% | 1.5% | 10.6% | 36.4% | 34.1% | 81.8% | 8.3% |
| Single region | 38 | 10.5% | 50.0% | 71.1% | 73.7% | 15.8% | 7.9% | 26.3% | 68.4% | 73.7% | 89.5% | 55.3% |
| Interregional route | 54 | 1.9% | 37.0% | 29.6% | 44.4% | 48.1% | 9.3% | 16.7% | 48.1% | 38.9% | 85.2% | 27.8% |
| Pan-European/multi-region | 29 | 34.5% | 72.4% | 41.4% | 79.3% | 86.2% | 48.3% | 37.9% | 79.3% | 44.8% | 96.6% | 55.2% |

What does not work:

- Practicality does not explain single-city print geography. Practical claims are relatively weak in single-city lines and are strongest in pan-European/multi-region lines.
- Vernacularity does not mean single-city. It is strongest in single-region print geography, especially Dou/Henrion-style regional vernacular printing.
- Books 1-6/books 1-6 plus solids do not define local print geography. They are strongest in pan-European/multi-region lines and single-region lines, not single-city lines.
- Method/order, augmentation, correction, and restoration do not map cleanly onto geographic reach. Restoration is high everywhere and therefore does not discriminate well. Method and correction are especially high in single-region and pan-European/multi-region lines, not uniquely local.
- Institutional mention is not a simple local-school signal. It appears in 38.6% of single-city matched editions, but it is much stronger in pan-European/multi-region lines, 86.2%.

What does work:

- Jesuit markers are the clearest correlate of wider observed print reach. They are very rare in single-city lines, 2/132 matched editions, weak in single-region and interregional-route lines, and strongest in pan-European/multi-region lines, 14/29 matched editions.
- At the author-line level, all 3 pan-European/multi-region lines have Jesuit markers: Dechales, Clavius, and Tacquet. By contrast, only 2/89 matched single-city author lines have Jesuit markers.
- The Jesuit pattern does not mean that every Jesuit-related line is pan-European, but it does suggest that Jesuit/institutional networks are unusually good at making a named Euclidean rendering reappear across multiple print environments.

Revised claim:

> Same-author renderings of the Elements divide into local, regional, routed, and pan-European/multi-region print-geographic types. These types do not correspond neatly to practical versus theoretical, vernacular versus Latin, short versus full Euclid, or methodizing versus restoring versus augmenting. The exception is the Jesuit/institutional network: Jesuit-marked lines are strongly associated with wider multi-city print reach, especially the pan-European/multi-region profiles of Dechales, Clavius, and Tacquet.

Interpretation:

- Practicality does not predict single-city print geography. It is relatively weak in single-city series, 11/133 matched editions, and strongest in multi-language mediation, 11/47 matched editions. This is largely Dechales-driven.
- Books 1-6/books 1-6 plus solids do not predict single-city print geography. They are more common in regional vernacular series, 18/28, and multi-language mediation, 25/47, than in single-city series, 45/133.
- Vernacular/non-Latin does not predict single-city print geography. It defines the regional vernacular profile and is also strong in multi-language mediation. Single-city series include non-Latin material, but not at a distinctive rate.
- Institutional mention is not a single-city signal. It appears at similar or higher rates in multi-city Latin institutional and multi-language mediation profiles. Regional vernacular series are the clear negative case: Dou/Henrion-style vernacular printing does not need institutional display on the title page.
- Jesuit markers do support the multi-city part of the hypothesis. They are very rare in single-city series, 2/133 matched editions, absent from regional vernacular series, and much stronger in multi-city Latin institutional and multi-language mediation profiles. At the author-line level, 5/14 multi-city Latin institutional lines and 2/9 multi-language mediation lines have any Jesuit marker, compared with only 2/90 single-city lines.

Revised claim:

> The tested predictors do not define a single "local" profile. Practicality, shorter book coverage, and vernacular language are more strongly tied to vernacular or multi-language mediation than to single-city seriality. Institutional and Jesuit display point in the opposite direction: they are more compatible with multi-city Latin or multi-language print reach. Single-city series are better explained by local print depth, format tradition, and city-specific pedagogical or scholarly markets, not by practicality or vernacularity alone.

## How This Changes The Presentation

Replace any broad claim that most authors/adapters were confined to one city with a corpus-specific print-geography claim:

> In the Elements corpus, observed print geography before 1705 is highly local in its reprint mechanics: about 73% of sequential reprint links remain in the same city and about 97% remain in the same language. But the print record is not immobile. A smaller number of routes place particular versions in multiple cities, especially Paris/Cologne, Lyon/Geneva, Urbino/Pesaro, Ingolstadt/Bologna, and Rotterdam/Utrecht. Translation metadata points especially to French as a key language of controlled transfer from Latin and Greek, and later into English.
