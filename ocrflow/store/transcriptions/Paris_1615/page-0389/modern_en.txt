4.  Straight lines incommensurable in
power are those of which the squares
have no common surface that can
measure them.

5.  This being so, it is evident that for every
straight line proposed, one will find infi-
nitely many straight lines commensurable, and infi-
nitely many incommensurable: some in length
and power; others in power only.
Now a straight line proposed is called
rational.

If one proposes some straight line of known and deter-
mined magnitude, it is called rational, because it is according to it that we
reason.
Dounot judged that this definition ought not to have place; and there-
fore rejected it. Which I judge he ought not to have done, seeing that he makes use
almost everywhere of this rational line mentioned in this de-
finition.

6  Lines commensurable with a rational
line, whether in length and power, or in
power only, are called rational.

7  And lines incommensurable with a
rational line are irrational,

If, comparing some straight line with the line taken as rational, and
upon which we reason, it is found commensurable with that
rational one, whether in length and power, or in power only,
such a line is also called rational; but if it is incommensurable with
that proposed rational line, both in length and in power, this
said line is called irrational.

8.  The square described on a rational line
is called rational.
