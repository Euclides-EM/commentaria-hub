Just as that line there, which is known and determined by
a certain quantity, is called rational; so also the square described upon
that line is called rational, because it is certain and
known; and the surfaces compared to that square are also called
rational, or else irrational, according as they shall be found com-
mensurable with that rational square, as is said in the two fol-
lowing definitions.

9.  Figures commensurable with the rational square
are also rational.
10. And those which are incommensurable with the
rational square are irrational and surd.
11. And the lines which can [produce] those irrational
figures are irrational and surd.

Now a line is said to be able [to produce] a figure when the square described
upon it is equal to that figure, since every square is the pow-
er of its root or of its side. Thus it has been said above that two
lines are commensurable in power, when not the lines,
but the squares of those lines, can be measured by one
and the same surface.

Now to these definitions we shall add (after Clavius) a de-
mand, and some common sentences, the use of which will be found
in this book.

DEMAND.

That one may multiply any magnitude
so many times that it exceeds any
magnitude of the same kind.

COMMON  SENTENCES.

1.  A magnitude measuring as many magnitudes
as one wishes, also measures the composite of those
[magnitudes].
