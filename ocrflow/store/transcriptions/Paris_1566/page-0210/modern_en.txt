Proem of the tenth [book] of Euclid’s Elements.

Euclid planned in his elements of Geometry to set before the minds of learners the nature of both kinds of quantities: of those, indeed, which can be so compared with one another that some part of the one may be called a part of the other, or the same; and of those which are so compared with one another that no part of the one can be called a part of the other. But what he had taught separately in the previous nine books—namely, that for any equal sections the quantities cut off, or when multiplied by any units, never produce equal magnitudes—he gathers in this tenth [book] in such an order as to make easier for the mind to perceive the origin and rise of this discrepancy. For in the first six [books], which looked to the understanding of conjoined quantities (that is, continuous), with many rudiments he more often gave to those grasping the affinities and precepts of discrete magnitudes; and Geometry indeed contains both natures of quantities, namely ratios of numbers that have [a ratio] and that do not have [a ratio]. But in the last three, which pertained to the knowledge of discrete quantities, he taught by laws drawn from numbers, by which he might open up the understanding of those quantities to the distinction of numbers (which is familiar to the senses). And since it is known that the distinction of numbers is generated from unity (which, repeated always, produces equal parts), it will follow that the single units of numbers are equal to one another, and hence that magnitudes expressed by numbers can at last be reduced to equal parts, namely to that which unity makes. For unity is a part of any number, but of no [unit] are there parts; and if to some magnitude of these units a magnitude be compared having to it a ratio not expressible by any numbers, it will follow that magnitudes composed of these distinct units by equal repetition have a ratio not expressible by any numbers: for parts of multiples have the same ratio by the fifteenth [proposition] of the fifth [book]. But since no distinction of quantity is left to the senses of mortals if numbers are absent, by which we express excess or indeed defect by the naming of a part or parts, which, generating in the intellect a knowledge of quantity, pronounce only what it is in the proposed magnitude of quantity. Therefore Euclid will set forth that comparisons of geometrical quantities have a twofold nature: namely that by which, when compared with one another, they are perceived to have a ratio of numbers, which he will call certain, on account of that discrete certainty of numbers, presenting to minds a determinate and constant knowledge of the proposed quantities (mutually compared); and that by which, when compared, they have a ratio which can fall between no numbers, and these he will therefore call uncertain, because, when compared with the certain, they are deprived of all distinction of relation, through the pressing lack of affinity of numbers; and finally, into however many equal segments (though unequal in number) each of those quantities be divided, [they are divided] infinitely. Nowhere will the same quantity of unity be able to measure both, for the two quantities measured by unity will always be unequal, though they be cut by any division. And since infinite inequalities of their units can be given, indeed from the diverse roots of numbers (and comparisons), we shall be compelled to conclude that infinite species of incommensurable magnitudes can be given (as we shall teach in 15.), among which no less will there intervene an uncertainty of comparison than we see to occur between them and a certain [magnitude]; and that indeed when we understand that they differ from one another in species.
