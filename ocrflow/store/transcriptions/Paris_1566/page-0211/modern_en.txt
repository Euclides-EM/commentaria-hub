of those numbering the prior quantity; since if that end, by some multiplication
of a decrement of unity, were to fall into some term of those unities, and not
into a division of unity, the quantities of that multiplication would (at least) both number
the magnitudes of the unities. Therefore their comparison would not be held beyond the powers of numbers,
but would be expressed by numbers. Since therefore it cannot be expressed by numbers, it lacks determination
and thus certainty: for the certainty of the quantity of a comparison depends on the expression of numbers.
Therefore a comparison which cannot be expressed by numbers deserves for this reason to be called uncertain:
and therefore the quantity which produces that comparison to another quantity can by the same rule be called
an uncertain quantity. Moreover, being deprived of every denomination, it becomes mute: because it has burst forth
into such wickedness as to attempt to dissolve that inviolate integrity of unity,
which admits no denomination of division.

But following that which Zambert says in the preliminary [matter], namely that Euclid’s
perspective exemplars, worn by moths and decay, lived on among the Socratic [people]. Indeed we can infer
that this tenth [book] of his, or some other, has suffered some damage. For Euclid
calls this proposed magnitude (which is a line) determinate by the word ἄλογον,
(which properly bears before it “certain” and “determinate” and “what can safely be said”) in the fifth
definition. But the sixth sets forth that all commensurable [magnitudes] with the same proposed determinate [one]
are likewise determinate by the same word ἄλογον, from the habit of commensuration indeed, because commensurable [things]
are [with] a determinate proposed [one], and they themselves will for that reason be determinate; but when the seventh
definition argues from privation, it departs from sense. For what “habit” adds, “privation” takes away.
For the sixth will say that because they were commensurable with determinate [ones], therefore they are determinate.
Therefore the seventh, because they themselves were incommensurable with determinate [ones], says that it necessarily follows
that they are indeterminate (with a privative negation prefixed). But why Theon put in place of this word ἄλογον,
to which truly ἄρρητον is opposed by privation, this ἄλογον, thinking it to be the opposite of the certain or determinate,
namely irrational, we cannot sufficiently wonder. But since it has nothing in common with reason, and moreover
[is] absurdities, for although he always uses this ἄλογον affirmatively, nevertheless wishing to set against it
an opposite, he posits ἄλογον throughout this whole tenth [book], which is no small inconvenience to learners.
