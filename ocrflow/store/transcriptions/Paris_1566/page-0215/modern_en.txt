we shall see hereafter. Wherefore we say that this quantity, as expressed by prior numbers, is incommensurable,
because it must be expressed by units which are wholly incommensurable with the prior units. And therefore the
numbers of this number will always be incommensurable with the numbers of that one. Since individual numbers
follow the nature of their units. Therefore we shall call magnitudes incommensurable, from the privation of a
common measure, not having the same kind of measure. Not otherwise than those lacking the same kind of
quantity, irrationals are rightly said, from the privation of ratio, by the third and fifth definition.

Third Definition.

Straight lines are commensurable in power, when the squares on them are
measured by the same area.

Fourth Definition.

But they are incommensurable, when no area can be a common measure
of the squares which are on them.

Since Euclid calls the power of a line the square on that line, or the same surface equal to this
square, he says therefore: that straight lines are commensurable in power (that is, that the squares on straight
lines, or surfaces equal to them, are commensurable) when the same area precisely by its repetition constitutes
each, as we said in the first definition concerning magnitudes in general. But here he says that any quantity is
measured by the same kind of quantity, and therefore that measure is expressed by numbers, so that at length
between them a denomination of ratio may fall, expressed by the power of numbers: but incommensurables, by
the opposite word, are to be explained by privation, since no area can be given as a common measure between
their squares. To these definitions we have added what we said before in general, following that, in the first of
the seventh definition, namely that the unit takes on that form of quantity when a number is to be applied. For
