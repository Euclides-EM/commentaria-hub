DEFINITIONS.

1  Commensurable magnitudes are those which
are measured by one and the same measure.

Thus the two straight lines, A & B,
being measured by one and the same line
C, are said to be commensurable. Thus also a line of 6 feet is commen-
surable with another line of 7 feet, because
they are both measured as well
by the line of one foot, as by that
of a half foot, of a third of a foot, &c.
likewise the surfaces, which are
measured by one and the same surface, are
said to be commensurable: likewise the bodies,
or solids which are measured by one and the same
body, are also said to be commensurable.

2  But incommensurable magnitudes are those which
have no common measure.

Such magnitudes are the diameter & the side of any square: for those mag-
nitudes have no common measure, as will be demonstrated in the last prop. of this
book. There are also several other incommensurable lines, namely those to which one
cannot give any common measure, many of which will be explained in this 10th
book; and it is taught by what ways they can be found: likewise, the super-
ficies are said to be incommensurable, and the solids incommensurable, which have no
common measure.
