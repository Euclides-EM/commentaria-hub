And the lines commensurable with the given one in length, and in
power, or indeed in power only, shall be called rational.
VII.
And the incommensurable ones shall be called irrational.
VIII.
And the square of the given straight line shall be called rational,
IX.
And the areas commensurable with it, rational.
X.
And the areas incommensurable, shall be named irrational.
XI.
And the straight lines which can produce incommensurable areas
shall be called irrational: and if they are squares, their sides;
but if rectilinear of another kind, those which describe the squares
equal to them.

THE COMMENTARY.

There are also certain common notions, of which Euclid makes use in this book, namely.

COMMON NOTIONS.
I.
Every magnitude multiplied can exceed every other magnitude
proposed of the same kind.
II.
Whatever magnitude measures another, will also measure that
which the said magnitude measures.
III.
Whatever magnitude measures the whole, and a part taken from
it, will also measure the remainder.
IIII.
Whatever magnitude measures two or more magnitudes, will likewise
measure that which is composed of them.

THEOREM I.  PROPOSITION I.

Two unequal magnitudes being proposed, if from the greater there be
cut off a part greater than the half, and from what remains likewise
there be cut off a part greater than the half, and this be done
always, in the end there will remain a certain magnitude, which will
be less than every proposed lesser magnitude.
