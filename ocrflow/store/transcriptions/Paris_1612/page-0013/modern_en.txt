DEFINITION I.

C O M M E N S U R A B L E magnitudes are said to be those which the same  
measure measures.

TWO magnitudes a and b are called commensurable because they have a common mea-  
sure c, which measures each of a and b.

FROM CLAVIUS.

IN THE SAME way commensurable are a line of 20 palms and a line of 12 palms, because those lines, both of one palm,  
and of half a palm, and of a third part of one palm measure them.  
Likewise surfaces are called commensurable, which one and the same surface measures.  
Also bodies, solids, commensurable, which the same solid body measures.

DEFINITION II.

I N C O M M E N S U R A B L E, however, are those for which it happens that no common measure  
can be found.

INCOMMENSURABLE magnitudes a and b, because between themselves they have no  
common measure, which can measure each.  
Again surfaces are called incommensurable, solids incommensurable, which admit no  
common measure.

FROM CLAVIUS.

SUCH magnitudes are the diameter of any square and its side, since they have no common measure,  
as is clear from the last proposition of this book.
