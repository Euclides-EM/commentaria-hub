Euclid calls lines which can [produce] spaces, or irrational squares, Irrational:
just as those squares also are called Irrational. But those spaces are irrational,
when they are incommensurable with the square on a Rational line; so that if those
spaces are squares, the lines from which those squares are described are Irrational.
    But if those spaces are not squares, but other rectilinear figures, the lines
which can [produce] those spaces are called Irrational.

FROM CLAVIUS.

BESIDES each one thing which Euclid notes, he also [uses] the true Commensurables by the term:
in length and in power, and in power only; and he almost always adds to those words:
commensurable and incommensurable. But scarcely ever separately these:
Rational and Irrational. For straight lines are called commensurable in length and in power, or in power
only. Likewise incommensurable in length and in power or in power only. But the straight lines Rational in length and in
power, or in power only, are Irrational in length and in power, or in power only. Which indeed, since Campanus did not
notice, provided occasion to many, that variously and obscurely in this 10th book, about commensurable lines, and incommensurable
ones, and also about Rational and Irrational, they have spoken.
    Moreover to these definitions we shall add one postulate, and certain pronouncements, whose use is found in this book.

Postulate or Petition.

FROM CLAVIUS.

IT IS POSTULATED, that any magnitude can be multiplied so many times, until
it exceeds any magnitude of the same kind.

FOR, two magnitudes of the same kind being proposed unequal, since the greater is not infinite,
but the lesser can be increased infinitely, it is evident that the lesser can be multiplied so many
times, until it surpasses the greater.
    This is also established from those things which we wrote in def. 5 of book 5. For there we said those magnitudes
are then at last judged to be of the same kind, when either can be so multiplied, that it exceeds the other.
And from the failure of this condition, we taught that a rectilinear angle, and an angle of contact, are of
different kinds.

Axioms or Pronouncements.

AXIOM I.

A MAGNITUDE measuring however many magnitudes, also measures the composite
from them.

AXIOM II.

A MAGNITUDE measuring any magnitude, also measures every
magnitude which that [magnitude] measures.

AXIOM III.

A MAGNITUDE measuring the whole magnitude, and the part taken away, also measures the
remainder.
