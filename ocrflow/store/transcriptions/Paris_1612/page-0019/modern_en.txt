THESE axioms, as they pertain to numbers, have been set forth by us in the last three
pronouncements of book 7. Wherefore since the same reasoning holds in magnitudes, there is
no reason that we should uselessly repeat their demonstrations here, especially since they can
be transferred hither word for word, only changing the word “number” into the word “magnitude”.

Theor. I.     Propos.1.

WITH two unequal magnitudes set forth, if from the greater there be taken away more than
the half, and from that which remains there be again taken away more than the half, and this
be always done: there will at length be left some magnitude which will be less than the lesser
proposed magnitude.

c        d      f        i
a        e  z   b

LET there be set forth two magnitudes a b, and c d, unequal, of which a b is the greater,
but c d the lesser. I say that if from a b there be taken away more than the half, namely a e,
and from the remainder again more than the half, namely e z, and this be always done, there
will at length be left some magnitude which is less than c d.
   For let c d be multiplied so many times until the magnitude made from that multiplication
exceed the magnitude a b, and let that magnitude be c i, which therefore is a multiple of c d,
next greater than a b.
   Then let c i be divided into parts equal to c d, and let those parts be c d, d f, f i, and let
there be taken away from a b more than the half, a c; and from the remainder e b again more
than the half, e z; and let this be done until the parts of the line a b are equal in number to
the parts of c i.
   Since therefore c i is greater than a b, and from c i there has been taken away c d, less than
its half, or certainly the half if c i is double of c d; but from a b there has been taken away a c,
more than its half, the remainder d i will be greater than the remainder e b. For since c i is
greater than a b, if from c i the half had been taken away, and from a b the half also taken away,
the remainders would not have been equal, but one would be greater than the other, namely the
remainder from c i greater than the remainder from a b.
   Therefore taking away from c i less than the half, or certainly the half, and from a b more
than the half, the remainder from c i will be greater than the remainder from a b.
   Then since d i is greater than e b, and there has been taken away from d i the half of it, d f,
or certainly less than the half; from e b there has been taken away e z, greater than the half; in
the same way the remainder f i will be greater than the remainder z b. And thus proceeding it is
easy to demonstrate that the last part of c i, namely f i, is greater than the last part of a b, namely
z b.
   But f i, the last part of the straight line c i, is equal to c d. Therefore the straight line c d is
greater than z b, the last part of a b.
   Wherefore by that subtraction there is left the remainder z b, which is less than c d, which
was to be shown.

SCHOLIUM FROM CLAVIUS.

MORE clearly than light from the demonstration of this Theorem it appears that two unequal
quantities set forth ought to be such that the lesser, when multiplied, can at length exceed the
greater. Which I noted with respect to proposition 16 of book 3, when I was treating with myself
concerning the angle of contact against Lactantius Ptolemy.
