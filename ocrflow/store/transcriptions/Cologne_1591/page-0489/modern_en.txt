EUCLID’S ELEMENTS
II.

INCOMMENSURABLES, however, are those for which it happens that no common measure
can be found.

SUCH magnitudes are the diameter of any square, and the side of the same; which
have no common measure, as will be shown by Euclid in the last proportion of this book. For they are
incommensurable lines, for which, namely, no common measure can be given; many of which
the Geometer explains in this book. Thus also both plane figures will be called incommen-
surable, and solids incommensurable, which admit no common measure.

III.

STRAIGHT lines are commensurable in power, when the squares on them measure the same
area.

OF straight lines, some are commensurable in length, which indeed some other line, as a
common measure, measures; of which sort are those which in the exposition of the definition we called simply
commensurable: but some are incommensurable in length, which, namely, no line, as a common
measure, measures. Again, of lines which are incommensurable in length, some are such that their squares
are commensurable; but others are so disposed that their squares also are incommensurable. Therefore those
lines incommensurable in length, whose squares are commensurable, will be called by Eu-
clid commensurable in power; because, namely, according to their powers; that is, according to their squares
(for, as we said at prop. 7 of book I, the power of any line is the square described on it)
they are commensurable, although the lines themselves (which are in length) are for that reason incommensurable.

BUT if anyone should ask why Euclid defined lines to be commensurable in power, and not
commensurable in length, it must be answered that lines commensurable in length have been sufficiently explained
above in the definition of commensurable magnitudes, since a line of this kind is measured by one and a common measure,
as has been said. But since lines commensurable in power cannot be measured by any common measure,
but only their squares measure the same area, therefore it was altogether necessary that they be explained by a proper
definition.

IIII.

INCOMMENSURABLES, however, are those for which, with respect to their squares, no area
which is a common measure of them can happen to be found.

THE remaining lines incommensurable in length, whose squares also are incommensurable,
he calls incommensurable in power.

BUT lines commensurable both in length and in power have this, as it were, connection between
themselves, and a connection, that lines commensurable in length are also commensurable in power; so that no
lines can be given commensurable in length, whose squares are not also commensurable; since
a square described from commensurable measures measures their squares as a common measure; whence this form
appears: let A B be intermediate, let it measure the straight lines

as will be shown in this book. Again, among lines incommensurable both in length and in power, this

unequal in power, since many lines are found incommensurable in length with one another, while yet in power, that
is, according to their squares, they are commensurable. Which things indeed will be clear from the corollary
to proposition 9 of this book.
