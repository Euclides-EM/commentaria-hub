OF straight LINES some are commensurable
in length, namely those which some other line, as a common
measure, measures; of which sort are those which in the exposition, defin.
1. we said were simply commensurable: but some are incommensurable
in length, which indeed no line, as a common
measure, measures. Again, of lines which are
incommensurable in length, some are of such a sort that their
squares are commensurable; but others are so disposed that also
their squares are incommensurable. Therefore those lines
incommensurable in length, whose squares are commen-
surable, are here by Euclid called commensurable in power;
because indeed according to their powers, that is, accord-
ing to their squares (for, as we said at prop.47. book 1,
the power of each line is the square described on it)
they are commensurable, although they themselves according to length are
altogether incommensurable.

BUT if anyone should ask why Euclid defined separately
lines commensurable in power, but not commensurable
in length; it must be answered, that lines commensurable in length are
more than sufficiently explained in the definition of commen-
surable magnitudes, &c. since lines of this sort a single common
measure measures, as has been said. But since lines
commensurable in power no common measure can measure,
but only their squares measure the same space,
therefore it was altogether necessary that they be explained
by a proper definition.
