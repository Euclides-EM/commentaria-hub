OF straight lines, some are commensurable
in length, which indeed some other line, as a common
measure, measures; of which sort are those which in the exposition of Def.
1 we said are simply commensurable: but some are incommensurable
in length, which namely no line, as a common
measure, measures. Again, of lines which are
incommensurable in length, some are of such a sort that their
squares are commensurable, but others are so disposed that even
their squares are incommensurable. Therefore those lines
incommensurable in length, whose squares are commens-
urable, are here called by Euclid commensurable in power;
because indeed according to their powers, that is, accord-
ing to their squares (for, as we said at Prop. 47 of Book 1,
the power of each line is the square described on it)
they are commensurable, although they themselves according to length are
altogether incommensurable.

BUT if anyone should ask why Euclid has defined separately
lines commensurable in power, but not those commensura-
ble in length; it must be answered that lines commensurable in length are sufficiently and more than sufficiently
explained in the definition of commensurable magnitudes, and that lines of this kind are measured by one common
measure, as has been said. But since lines commensurable in
power cannot be measured by any common measure,
but only their squares measure the same space,
therefore it was altogether necessary that they be explained by a proper definition.
