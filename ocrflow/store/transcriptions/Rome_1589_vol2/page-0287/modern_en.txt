IF some straight line of known magnitude be proposed, to
which, of all other [lines] that are compared with it, some are
commensurable in length and in power, and some indeed in
power only: likewise some are incommensurable with it in
length only; and some in length and in power, as will be
demonstrated in various places in this tenth book. For Eu-
clid does not wish, from what has been said, to be shown, or
gathered (although his words may seem to sound thus) that
these things are so: but he only indicates to
us, and in some manner hints, that some of the lines can be
said to be commensurable with the proposed straight line in
length and in power, and some in power only, &c: which
indeed in no way follows from what has been said, but will
be demonstrated in what follows, as we have said.

BUT that proposed line, by reference to which others are
commensurable or incommensurable, is called in Greek
ῥητὴ; but in Latin Rational; because it is always set down as
known, and known, whereas the others, compared with it, are not always
known, although each separately always exists as a definite magnitude
and known; since it can be divided into any number of equal parts.

AND those commensurable with it either in
length and power, or in power
only, [are] Rational.

HE TEACHES that other lines, commensurable with that
which is called Rational in whatever way, are also called Rational;
not indeed by position, as that [one], but because, compared with it,
they are found commensurable with it either in length and power,
or in power only—.
