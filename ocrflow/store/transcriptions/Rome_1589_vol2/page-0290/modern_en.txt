It is concluded, however, that lines which can produce areas incommen-
surable with the square on a Rational line ought to be called Irrationa-
l, from the 7th definition; just as above from the 6th definition we conclude
that lines which can produce areas commensurable with the same square
on a Rational line ought to be called Rational. For lines
which can produce those incommensurable areas are in power in-
commensurable with the Rational line, from the 4th definition. Since therefore all
lines incommensurable in power are also incommen-
surable in length, as we said at the 4th definition, and it will be demon-
strated in the corollary of proposition 2, it is clear, from the 7th definition, that those
lines which can produce areas incommensurable with the square on a Ratio-
nal line are called Irrational.

Nor is it to be passed over that Euclid and the an-
cient Geometers almost always add these words: in length and in power, and in
power only, to these words: commen-
surable and incommensurable; but scarcely, and very rarely, to these:
Rational and Irrational. For lines are rightly said to be com-
mensurable in length and in power, or in power only;
likewise incommensurable in length and in power, or in length
only; but less rightly Rational in length and in
power, or in power only, or Irrational in length and
power, or in length only. Which indeed, because
Campanus did not notice, provided an occasion for many, that
variously and obscurely in this 10th book, concerning commensurable lines,
and incommensurable, and also Rational and Irrational,
they have spoken.
