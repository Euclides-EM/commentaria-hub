V I I.

But let those incommensurable with this be called
irrational.

SINCE in the preceding definition Euclid called those lines
which are commensurable with the proposed Rational straight line
in length and power, or in power only, “Rational”;
it is clear that here he calls “Irrational” those which are
incommensurable with the proposed Rational straight line in every
way, namely in length and in power, but not in length only.
From which it is also evident that the square root of this number
20, or 1000, etc., or (which is the same) the line whose square is
20, or 1000, etc., cannot be called Irrational, since it is not
incommensurable in length and power, but in length only, with the
proposed Rational line.

V I I I.

And the square which is made from the proposed
straight line shall be called Rational.

Just as those lines which are set down as definite and known
are called Rational: so also the square described on it is called
Rational; because it too is definite and known, and in comparison
with it other surfaces are said to be commensurable or
incommensurable.

I X.

And those commensurable with this indeed,
Rational.
