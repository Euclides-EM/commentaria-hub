V I I.

But let those which are incommensurable with this be
called Irrational.

SINCE in the preceding definition Euclid called those lines
which are commensurable in length and in power, or which are
commensurable in power only, with the proposed Rational straight
line, Rational; it is clear that here he calls Irrational those which
are incommensurable with the proposed Rational straight line in
every way, namely in length and in power, but not in length only.
From which it is also evident that the square root of this number
20. or 1000. &c. or (which is the same) the line whose square is
20. or 1000. &c. cannot be called Irrational, since it is not
incommensurable in length and in power, but in length only, with
the proposed Rational line.

V I I I.

And let the square which is made from the proposed
straight line be called Rational.

Just as that line which is set down as determinate and known
is called Rational: so also the square described on it is called
Rational; because it too is determinate and known, and in
comparison with it other surfaces are said to be commensurable
or incommensurable.

I X.

And those commensurable with this indeed,
Rational.
