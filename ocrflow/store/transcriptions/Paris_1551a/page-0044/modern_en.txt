OUR PURPOSE is this: to set forth this tenth book of the
Elements (whose understanding most people suspect to be very
difficult, and indeed not possible without the help of that part of
arithmetic which, in the writings of many, is illustrated and is called
Algebra) without any irrational numbers at all being mentioned; to
show it to be not only not very difficult, but even very easy, if one
brings an attentive mind and one instructed by knowledge of the
preceding books of Euclid; and moreover to need no external
science of anyone, much less the demonstrations of algebra; but to
depend only on Euclid’s own demonstrations, and on an order most
familiar to him.  But in order that this definition may be understood
more clearly, I think it must first be explained, if anything in it seems
to be somewhat obscure. for thus the dialecticians prescribe.  When
therefore it is said that some measure measures another by a
magnitude: this is understood first, that that measure be less than
that which it measures, or at least equal to it. for a greater can in no
way measure a lesser. Next, that that measure, once taken, if it shall
be equal, or if it shall be less, repeated several times: may exactly
