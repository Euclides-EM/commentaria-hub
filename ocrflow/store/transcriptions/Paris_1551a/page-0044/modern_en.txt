COMMENSURABLE magnitudes
are called those which the same measure
measures.

IT IS PROPOSED for us to show that this tenth book of the
Elements (whose understanding most people suspect to be very
difficult, and indeed not possible without the help of that part of
arithmetic which, illustrated by the writings of many, they call
Algebra) can be set forth without any mention at all of irrational
numbers, not only as not very difficult, but even as very easy, if
someone brings an attentive mind and one instructed by knowledge
of the earlier books of Euclid; and that it needs no one’s external
knowledge, much less the demonstrations of algebra; but depends
only on Euclid’s own demonstrations, and on an order most familiar
to him.  But in order that this definition may be understood more
clearly, I think it should first be explained, if anything in it seems
to be somewhat obscure; for thus the dialecticians prescribe.  Since
therefore it is said that some measure measures another magnitude:
this is understood first, that that measure is less than the thing it
measures, or at least equal to it. for a greater [measure] can in no
way measure a lesser. Next, that that measure, taken once, if it will
be equal, or if it will be less, repeated several times: may exactly
