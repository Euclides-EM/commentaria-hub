Understand that point about lines incommensurable in
length and in power. For lines incommensurable in power only,
such that the same lines are not also incommensurable in length,
cannot exist. Nor should you suppose that Euclid here is treating
of lines incommensurable in length only,
but commensurable in power. For he has lately placed these among
the ῥητὰς, that is, the rational. But here he wishes lines
incommensurable in every respect, that is, in power and in
length, to be called ἄλογος. And since this was not so understood
by Campanus, the man seems to have brought forward a cause of the error,
which he himself also handed down to posterity, as we shall say
presently. What ῥητὸν and ἄῤῥητον are, has been said before. But as
to this term ἄλογοι, it may be learned from the commentaries of Proclus
that ῥητὸν and ἄῤῥητον are opposed by privation,
so that ἄῤῥητον is contrary to τῷ ῥητῷ. But ἄλογον is more often
used in place of this word ἄῤῥητον, although ἄλογοι
and ἄῤῥητον are the same. The one is from ἀπὸ τῆς ἀριθμῆς,
the other from ἀπὸ τῆς λέξεως, which signify the same.
Therefore an ἄλογος line will be one whose ratio, its
length compared to the length of τῆς ῥητῆς, that is, of the line itself
in the first place, and by supposition rational, cannot be expressed
by any numbers. Nor think that ἄλογοι are so called by privation
of τῆς λόγου, that is, of proportion. For there is a certain proportion
of alogar lines among themselves not indeed wholly unknown to us,
still less does it escape the knowledge of nature:
as some wish; otherwise Euclid would in vain have undertaken that work,
indeed in a book in which he does nothing else
than teach the affections of those lines, and the proportions
which those ἄλογοι lines hold among themselves. But they are called
