One thing is what I think should be noticed in this place. When
  he had said that irrational [magnitudes] are those which are incommensurable and inexpressible,
  he wished to add concerning the lines describing those incommensurable [magnitudes],
  and he likewise called them irrational: although
  earlier, when he was speaking about those commensurable to the rational [magnitude], he said nothing
  about the lines describing those commensurable [magnitudes]. But
  he omitted this for this reason, because he seemed to himself to have done enough,
  and to have hinted at these, when he was speaking about lines commensurable to the rational [magnitude],
  under those words, “or in power only.”
  For the power of a line is its square, as we said before.
  But if the square shall have been commensurable
  to the rational [magnitude], the line also which can produce that square
  will be commensurable at least in power. And so, by the definition
  of lines commensurable to the rational [magnitude], it will also be [so].
  There exists a little book under the name of Aristotle, On indivisible lines,
  which indeed is understood easily, from many passages of his, not to be Aristotle’s:
  yet its learning makes it so that it ought to be attributed
  to someone from that noble and learned family of the Peripatetics.
  In it you will read many things conducive to the understanding of these principles.
  Of which we will note this one thing,
  which can seem to contain almost the chief explanation of the whole matter:
  that the commensurability and incommensurability of magnitudes with one another
  depends indeed on the nature of the magnitudes themselves; but rationality
  and irrationality come about by position, because that which in the first place
  is thus called a line—namely rational—becomes such by position.
