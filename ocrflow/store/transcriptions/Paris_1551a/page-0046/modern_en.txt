[uncertain: however there will be] one who reproaches me for pursuing matters too minute: let him know that our plan is this, that I may render this book, not so much difficult by its own nature as by ignorance of first principles, very easy to understand for those who will wish to explore this most beloved place of geometry.
And indeed, I think no one will doubt that very many have fallen shamefully into the greatest errors through the fault of their own imprudence and through a perverse understanding of the first principles, small as they are—provided only that, reading our work, he will have attained understanding of the things themselves. But of these matters enough; let us return to our path. We say that this definition of commensurable magnitudes, or if you prefer to call it a principle, is plainly understood by a certain analogy from that passage of Campanus. For what is said in numbers, of some numbers measuring other numbers: understand the same or something similar in magnitudes, of which we say that one is measured by another. And that this may be understood more plainly, let us take an example in one kind of magnitude. Let there be two lines a
and b, which if they are commensurable, there will also be some measure common to both, which is c. For that line c repeated twice equals precisely line b; but repeated three times, line a, and it also precisely equals.
