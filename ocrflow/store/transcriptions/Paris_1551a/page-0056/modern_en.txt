not on the nature of the magnitudes themselves; which
however is otherwise in commensurability and incommensurability.
And there are certain common conceptions of the mind,
which Euclid omitted here, both because
they are infinite, and because indeed they are of such a kind that each
can subject them to the mind by a certain small attention,
so that the place itself will seem to demand [them]; from which,
however, let us not omit those which will be suitable to particular
places, such as are found in the demonstrations
of the first and second theorem.

Campanus also adds this to the principles of this book,
That any quantity can be multiplied so many times, that
it exceeds any quantity of the same kind. Which
indeed he did rightly. For by the help of this principle at once
the demonstration of the first theorem of this book is used. But concerning
that magnitude he says here, which geometers and other mathematicians treat,
so that it can increase to infinity. In this place it seemed good to add
that, although others [do so] obscurely,
