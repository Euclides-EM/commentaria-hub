Theorem 1.  Proposition 1.

If from two proposed unequal quantities, from the greater there be taken away
more than the half, and from the remainder again there be taken away more than
the half, and then proceeding in the same way, finally
it is necessary that there remain a quantity smaller than the proposed
smaller.

[uncertain: and thus it is done more and more by hand.]

Let the two unequal quantities be a and b.c, and let b.c be the greater. I say that so
many times more than the half of b.c can be taken away (or of the remainder
of it) as will be necessary that there remain a quantity smaller than a. And to
show this let a be multiplied so many times that by such number it exceeds b.c, and let
d.e.f be the multiple of it greater than b.c; therefore there shall be taken away
from b.c more than the half, which shall be b.g, and again from the remainder (which is e.b.c)
there shall be taken away more than the half, which shall be g.h, and this again shall be done so many times
until b.c be divided into as many parts as many times a is contained in
d.e.f. Now I say that the last remainder (which in this place is e.h.c) is smaller than a.
And to make this clear let h.c be multiplied by as much as a is contained
in d.e.f, and let the multiple of it be K.l.m, because then each of the parts
of the true quantity of K.l.m is equal to l.h.c; it follows that K is less than b.g, and
l.m less than g.h. But because m is equal to l.h.c (by the convention) K.l.m will be
less than b.c, by which it will also be less than d.e.f. And I say therefore
that d.e.f is to a as K.l.m to h.c; and since d.e.f is greater than K.l.m it follows
(by the fourteenth proposition of the fifth book) that a is greater
than h.c, which is the proposition. And the same follows if from the greater there be taken away
the half, and again from the remainder the half, and thus to proceed so many times until
the greater be divided into as many parts as many times the smaller is contained in
any of its multiples exceeding as much as one wishes the greater of
the proposed. But it must be noted that in this one sees it to contradict the sixteenth
proposition of the third book, which proposes the angle of the tangent
to be less than any proposed angle contained by two straight lines, for
since any angle obtained from straight lines, from it we shall take away
more than the half, and likewise from the remainder we shall take away more than the half, and it is seen to be
necessary to be able to do this so many times that there remain a rectilinear angle
smaller than the angle of the tangent, of which thing the sixteenth proposition
of the third book concludes the opposite; but these angles are not univocal
because the curved and the straight are not similarly of one and the same kind. Nor
can it be known that the angle of the tangent is taken away so many times that it
exceeds, whatever it may be, a rectilinear angle, which thing is necessary as is made
manifest by the demonstration had above; therefore to this it is also
clear (since the consequent is followed from the antecedent) that any
rectilinear angle is greater than infinite angles of the tangent.
