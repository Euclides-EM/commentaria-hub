DEFINITIONS.
I.

Commensurable are called the magnitudes, when
they are measured by one and the same measure.

I I.

Incommensurable are said to be those magnitudes, of which
no common measure is found.

I I I.

Straight lines are commensurable in power, when
the same space measures their squares,

EVERY square is called the power of its side: and for
this reason, in the things to come, when the power
of a straight line shall be mentioned, we shall understand,
that is, as if we were to say the square of that straight line;
and if for example it shall be said, that of the straight lines
A, & B, in power the one is double of the other, it shall not
be understood that the straight line A is double of the straight line B,
but that the power of the straight line A is double of the power
of the straight line B, that is, that the square of
the straight line A is double of the square of the straight line
B. And the like shall be understood if it shall be said triple, or quadruple
&c. This being supposed, Euclid explains in the first
definition, what we ought to understand by commensurable
quantities, and says, that magnitudes then are
called commensurable, when they are measured by one and the same quantity.
