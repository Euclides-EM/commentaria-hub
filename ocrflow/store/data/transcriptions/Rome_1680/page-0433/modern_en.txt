VIII.
The square of the aforesaid Rational [magnitude], to which
all the others are compared, is also called Rational.

IX.
All the planes which are commensurable with the aforesaid
square are likewise called Rational.

X.
And those planes which are incommensurable with that same
aforesaid square are called Irrational.

XI.
Then the straight lines whose squares are incommensu-
rable with that same aforesaid square are called Irrationa-
l; as also those straight lines whose squares are equal
to the planes which are incommensurable with that same
aforesaid square are likewise said to be incommensura-
ble,

For example let AB be that straight line, established as known, with which all
the others are to be compared; AB will be, as has been said, that straight line
which we call Rational, whose square, as Euclid wishes in the eighth definition,
will be called Rational; and all the planes which will be commensurable with
the square of AB by some explanation in the 9th definition, will likewise be
called Rational. But those planes which will be incommensurable with the
square of AB, according to the sense of the tenth definition, will be called
Irrational. Finally the straight lines whose squares are incommensurable with
the square of AB are said to be irrational with respect to the straight line AB;
and those straight lines whose squares are equal to those planes which are
incommensurable with the square of AB, according to the tenor of the 11th
definition, are said to be Irrational with respect to the straight line AB.

I add, as P. Clavius does, a single postulate with some Axioms, of which
Euclid makes use in this Element.

POSTULATE.
It is taken as granted, that of two unequal magnitudes
of the same kind, the lesser can be multiplied so many
times, until there results a magnitude which exceeds the
greater.
