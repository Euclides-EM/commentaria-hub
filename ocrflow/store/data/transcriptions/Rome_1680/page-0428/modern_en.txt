that straight lines are commensurable in length when they are measured
by the same straight line; and planes are also commensurable when
the same space measures them; and in a similar way this first definition
can be applied to all particulars. He then explains in the second definition
which are the incommensurable quantities, and, keeping the same generality
in this as in the first, he calls incommensurable magnitudes those of which
no common measure is found; and this also, as is said
of the first definition, can be applied to all particulars, calling
straight lines incommensurable in length when no straight line
measures them; and planes also are said to be incommensurable when no
space is found that measures them; and in a similar way one can make the application to all
the other kinds of quantity.

Having set forth the knowledge of commensurable and incommensurable
quantities, thus in general, Euclid passes to the particular, and with the third definition
explains what we ought to understand when it is said, straight lines commensurable
in power; and he says that then straight lines will be said to be commensurable in
power when their squares are measured by the same space; as
for example: Let the straight lines be set out
AB, CD, whose squares, or
let us say powers, are AE, CF, which
are conceived as of such magnitudes
that AE divided into equal spaces,
noted X, and CF divided into equal spaces
noted Y, each of the spaces Y is
equal to each of the spaces X; then let
another plane be understood as Z, equal
to one of the spaces X, or Y; in such a case the space Z will measure exactly
the square AE, without anything being left over; and the same plane Z will measure
exactly the square CF, without anything being left over: from which the plane
Z will be a common measure of the two powers AE, CF, and by the first definition
the two powers AE, CF will be commensurable; according to such a hypothesis
the straight lines AB, CD, which are sides of those commensurable powers, are called
straight lines commensurable in power, so that whether the straight lines are or
are not commensurable in length, when their squares have a
common measure, those straight lines are said to be commensurable in power. And
note, that if the squares, or powers AE, CF are commensurable, and their sides
AB, CD are also commensurable in length, the same straight lines AB,
CD will be called commensurable in length, and will also be called
commensurable in power; but if the squares AE, CF are commensurable, and
their sides AB, CD are not commensurable in length, then the straight lines AB,
CD are said to be only commensurable in power.
