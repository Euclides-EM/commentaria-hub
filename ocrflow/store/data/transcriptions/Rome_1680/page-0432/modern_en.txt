For a clearer explanation one must reflect, that, since one must measure some
proposed space, this, as is common usage, is done in no other way than with
the help of some known measure, divided into palms, feet, and the like: and
because these palms, or feet, are not the same in all countries, but are of
different lengths, it is manifest that the divisions of such measures are
arbitrary; and not only the divisions are arbitrary, but the names of the same
measures are also arbitrary: since in some places their measures are called
Arms, Cubits &c. and in others they are called Canes, Chains, Perches, Paces,
and the like. Now, since Euclid must not restrict himself to one of the
aforesaid, or other particular measures, in order to speak in general of every
measure, whether it is used, or can be used, he intends to divide any straight
line into as many equal parts as one wishes, in such a way that this straight
line can represent all the measures that are used, and as many others as by
man’s choice can be used; and this is that straight line which he calls
Rational, so that under this word Rational we must understand not only all the
measures that are actually used in every country, but also those which by our
choice can be used. And because, when with some used measure one measures some
space, nothing else is done, except the comparison between the thing measured
and that with which it is measured; as for example if any measure AB were
proposed, whether it be a perch, or cane, or chain, or arm, or pace &c. which
we shall always call Rational, and with this one had to measure the length DE;
this operation is nothing else than to see how many times DE contains the
measure AB; or how many parts of DE of those which are in AB; which is a simple
comparison, which is made between the length DE, and the proposed measure AB.
Hence it is that Euclid calls Rational that line, to which all the others are
compared, and it is the same as saying, with which all the others are measured.

Note, that when the line is established, which we call Rational, as AB, or
another, which is arbitrarily divided into equal parts, each of those equal
parts, as BC, because it is single, is called Unity. And because, as has been
said, the Rational AB is a certain determined measure, to which all the other
straight lines are compared; therefore each of the equal parts which are in AB
is considered as a determined unity; indeed in the comparisons to be made
between the Rational and the other magnitudes, the knowledge of the single
unity C B suffices; it mattering little whether the Rational consists of a
single unity, or of more unities, equal to DB; since the Rational can be made
long or short, as one wishes; and it is the same thing to say that DE consists,
for example, of a thousand of those parts, which in AB are ten; as to say that
the straight line DE consists of a thousand unities, or parts equal to CB.
