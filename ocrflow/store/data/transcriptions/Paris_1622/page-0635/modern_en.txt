7.    And the lines incommensurable with
this rational line, shall be called
irrational.

Since in the preceding definition Euclid has called the lines commensurable in length and power, or in power only, with the proposed rational [line], rational, it is manifest by this definition that those which are incommensurable with the proposed rational [line] in length and power, and not in length only, are irrational.

8.    And the square described on the proposed
rational [line], shall be called rational.

Just as the line which is set down as known and determined is called rational: in the same way the square described on it is called rational, inasmuch as it is also known and certain, and in comparison with it the other surfaces are called commensurable or incommensurable.

9.    And the figures commensurable with this
rational square, shall be called rational.
