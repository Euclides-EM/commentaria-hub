Preface of the tenth [book] of Euclid’s Elements.

Euclid planned, in his Elements of Geometry, to set before the minds of learners the nature of both kinds of quantities: of those, indeed, which can be so compared with one another that some part of one may be called a part of the other, or the same; and of those which are so compared with one another that no part of one can be called a part of the other. But those quantities, cut off by any equal sections, or multiplied by any units, never produce equal magnitudes—these he taught separately in the previous nine books; in this tenth [book] he will gather them in order, so that the origin and rise of this discrepancy may be made easier for minds to perceive. For in the first six, which looked to the understanding of commensurable quantities (that is, of continuous ones), he more often, with many rudiments, taught to those grasping the affinities and precepts of discrete magnitudes; for Geometry contains each nature of quantities, namely ratios of numbers that have [a ratio], and that do not have [a ratio]. But in the last three, which pertained to the knowledge of discrete quantities, he taught by laws derived from numbers, by which he might open up the understanding of those quantities by the discreteness of numbers (which is familiar to the senses). And since it is known that the discreteness of numbers is generated from the unit (which, repeated, always produces equal parts), it will follow that the individual units of numbers are equal to one another, and thence that magnitudes expressed by numbers can at last be reduced to equal parts, namely to that which the unit makes. For the unit is a part of any number, but of no parts; but if a magnitude is compared to one of these units, having to it a ratio not expressible by any numbers, it will follow that magnitudes compared from these different units by equal multiplication have a ratio not expressible by any numbers: for parts of equimultiples, by the fifteenth of the fifth [book], have the same ratio. But since no discreteness of quantity is left to the senses of mortals if numbers are absent—by which we express excess or indeed defect through the naming of a part or parts—those [numbers], generating in the intellect the knowledge of quantity, pronounce only what it is in the proposed magnitude of quantity. Therefore Euclid will explain that comparisons of geometrical quantities have a twofold nature: namely that by which, when compared with one another, they are perceived to have a ratio of numbers, which he will call certain, on account of that discrete certainty of numbers, presenting to minds a determinate and constant knowledge of the proposed quantities (mutually compared); and that by which, when compared, they have a ratio which can fall between no numbers, and these he will therefore call uncertain, because, compared with the certain, they lack all discreteness of relation, through the pressing scarcity of affinity with numbers; and finally, into however many equal segments (though unequal in number) each of those quantities may be divided, [it is] infinitely. Nowhere will the same quantity of unit be able to measure both; for the two units measuring those quantities will always be unequal, though they be cut by any division. And since infinite inequalities of their units can be given, perhaps indeed from diverse roots or comparisons of numbers, we shall conclude that infinitely many species of incommensurable magnitudes can be given (as we shall teach in 15.), among which no less will there intervene an uncertainty of comparison than we see to occur between them and a commensurable [magnitude]; that indeed when we understand that they differ from one another in species.
