who have tried to draw out the denominations of these uncertain things from the denominations of

forth the proposed certain things by numbers, which, multiplied, joined, or varied by some other
arithmetical operation, would produce a number lacking a root; and this root—whether square, or
cubic, or “censical,” or whatever it may be—they impose as the denomination of these uncertain
things (when compared with the proposed certain thing), so that as nearly as possible, from numbers
denominating a certain quantity, they may be able to extract the denominations of uncertain things.
But these numbers they call surds, algebraic, radical, or uncertain, or improperly irrational (following
the earlier error); which we call hypothetical rather than essential, because they are taken only from
a hypothesis, deprived of all essence and denomination. Yet by their service we obtain better ratios
of certain to uncertain things, hitherto cultivated by human measures, as we make clear by an example:
a straight line A B being cut in extreme and mean ratio at G, through the units of A B, which are 12,
let us find A G as nearly as can be done. We shall draw (from the eleventh of the second) A B into
itself, making 144, and half of A B, namely B, into itself, making 36, which together make 180; and
the square root of these, minus B, makes A G. Therefore they will denominate the greater segment
A G. The root of 180 minus B, which root (since it is not among numbers) is deprived of all
denomination; but if it must be expressed as nearly as possible: we shall say that when A B is twelve,
A G is 7 and 1/2, approximately greater, or approximately less, 7 and 11/17. But the remainder B G
occupies the remainder of the 12. All which suits rather the service than the demonstration. We
therefore shall carry through what we have begun.
