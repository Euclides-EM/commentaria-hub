From Euclid, as in Campanus.                 Proposition  1

I  S
CAMPANUS
I  With two unequal quantities set out, let more than half be
taken away from the greater, and again from the remainder let more than half be
taken away, and thereafter likewise in the same way; it is necessary that at length,
a quantity less than the lesser set out be left.
Let there be two unequal quantities a & b, b c the greater: I say that so many times
more than half can be taken away from b c and from its remainder, that it will be necessary
that the remaining quantity be less than a. For let a be multiplied so many times until it exceed b,
and let its multiple be d e f, greater than b c. Let there be taken away therefore from b c more than half,
which is b d. And again from the remainder which is g e, more than half, which is g h; and let this also be done
so many times until b c is divided into as many parts as many times as a is contained in d e f.
I say then that the last remainder, which is h c, is less than a. For let h c be multiplied
as many times as a has been multiplied in d e f, and let its multiple be k l m. Therefore each one
of the quantities k l, m is equal to h c; it follows that k l is less than
b g, and also l, m less than g h: but since m is equal to h c, it will by conception be that k l m
is less than b c, therefore less than d e f. And since d e f is to a as k l m to h c, and d
e f is greater than k l m, it follows by 5 of the fifth, that a is greater than h c, which is the thing proposed.
And the same follows if from the greater half be taken away and again from the remainder
half, and this be done so many times until the greater is divided into as many parts as many times as
the lesser is contained in any of its multiples, the greater of the set out exceeding by however much.

CAMPANUS’ annotation.  But it must be observed that this proposition
seems to contradict the fifteenth of the third, proposing that the angle of contact is less
than any angle contained by two straight lines. For with any
rectilinear angle set out, if from it more than half be taken away, and again from the remainder more than half, it seems necessary
that this can be done so many times until a rectilinear angle less than an angle of
contact is left, the opposite of which is syllogized in the third. But these are not univocal angles,
for the curved and the straight are not simply of the same genus. But neither is the angle of
contact to be taken so many times as to exceed any rectilinear [angle], which is
necessary (as is clear from the foregoing demonstration) for this, that the consequent follow from the antecedent.
It is plain therefore that also any rectilinear angle is greater than infinitely many
angles of contact.
