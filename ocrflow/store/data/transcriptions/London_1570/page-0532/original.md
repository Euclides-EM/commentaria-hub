This propoſition teacheth, that in continuall quantity, which the 3. of the ſecond taught in numbers.
[/Margin]

{dropcap:S|lines=3|style=decorated|decoration="foliate ornament"}Vppoſe that the three commenſurable magnitudes geuen be A, B, C. It is required of theſe three magnitudes to finde out the greateſt common meaſure. Take (by the former propoſition) the greateſt common meaſure of the two magnitudes A, B, and let the ſame be D. Now then this magnitude D either meaſureth the third magnitude, which is C, or not. Firſt let it meaſure C.

[Margin]
Conſtruction.
[/Margin]

[Margin]
Two caſes in this Propoſition. The firſt caſe.
[/Margin]

And foraſmuch as D meaſureth C, and it meaſureth alſo the magnitudes A, B: therfore D meaſureth the three magnitudes A, B, C. Wherfore D is a common meaſure vnto the magnitudes A, B, C. And it is manifeſt, that it is the greateſt common meaſure. For no magnitude greater then D can meaſure the magnitudes A, B, C. For if it be poſſible let the magnitude E being greater then the magnitude D, meaſure the magnitudes A, B, C. And foraſmuch as E meaſureth the magnitudes A, B, C, it meaſureth the two firſt magnitudes A B. Wherfore it ſhall alſo (by the Corollary of the former propoſition) meaſure the greateſt common meaſure of the magnitudes A, B which is D, namely, the greater ſhall meaſure the leſſe, which is impoſſible.

[Margin]
The ſecond caſe.
[/Margin]

But now let D not meaſure the magnitude C. Firſt I ſay that the magnitudes C, D, are commenſurable. For foraſmuch as the magnitudes A, B, C are commenſurable, ſome magnitude ſhall meaſure them, which ſhall alſo meaſure the magnitudes A B taken a part. Wherfore it ſhall alſo (by the corollary going before) meaſure the greateſt common meaſure of A, B, that is, D: and (by ſuppoſition) it meaſureth the magnitude C. Wherfore that ſaid magnitude ſhall meaſure the magnitudes C and D. Wherfore the magnitudes C, D are commenſurable.

[Margin]
A Lemma neceſſary to be proued before he fall to the demonſtration of this caſe.
[/Margin]

Take by the third of this tenth, their greateſt common meaſure, & let the ſame be the magnitude E. And foraſmuch as E meaſureth D, but D meaſureth the magnitudes A, B. Wherfore E alſo meaſureth the magnitudes A, B, and it alſo meaſureth the magnitude C. Wherfore E is a common meaſure to the three magnitudes A, B, C. I ſay alſo that it is the greateſt common meaſure. For if it be poſſible, let there be a magnitude, namely, F, greater then the magnitude E. And let F meaſure the three magnitudes A, B, C. And foraſmuch as F meaſureth the magnitudes A, B, C, it alſo meaſureth the two firſt magnitudes A, B. Wherfore (by the corollary going before) it ſhall alſo meaſure the greateſt common meaſure of the magnitudes A, B. But the greateſt common meaſure of the magnitudes A, B, is D. Wherfore F meaſureth D, and it alſo meaſureth C. Wherfore F meaſureth the magnitudes C D. Wherfore F ſhall alſo meaſure the greateſt common meaſure of the magnitudes C, D. But the greateſt common meaſure of the magnitudes C, D, is E. Wherfore F meaſureth E, namely, the greater, the leſſe, which is impoſſible. Wherfore no magnitude greater then E meaſureth the magnitudes A, B, C. Wherfore E is the greateſt common meaſure of the magnitudes A B C. If D do not meaſure the magnitude C. But if D do meaſure C, then is D the greateſt common meaſure. Wherfore three magnitudes commenſurable being geuen, there is found their greateſt common meaſure: which was required to be done.
