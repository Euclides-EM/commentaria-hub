DEFINITIONS.
I.

Commensurable magnitudes are said to be those
which the same measure measures.

A
EUCLID has completed in the preceding three
books those things which pertain to numbers, although it is sufficiently
useful for understanding geometrical matters: Now in this tenth book
we proceed to the discussion of commensurable and incommensurable
lines, for the sake of which we said above that the treatment of numbers
was undertaken by him. For without knowledge of these lines, many
magnitudes, both solid and plane, can neither be perfectly understood,
nor, when the matter is inserted, be brought to work and use; because
the sides of most of them are incommensurable: which can also be said
of the planes themselves and the solids, since these too very often are
incommensurable, as we shall demonstrate at the end of this book.
But the plan of this book is more obscure because of difficulties, on
account of the obscurity of the lines about which it disputes; yet I shall
strive with all my diligence in this, that from those things which up to
now have been demonstrated by Euclid, it may be rendered so plain and
easy that without much labor it can be grasped by anyone who has
nevertheless correctly understood the demonstrations of the preceding
books. For I cannot go into the opinion of those who think that for that
understanding there is necessary that part of Arithmetic which sets forth
a discourse about the roots of numbers, both rational and irrational, as
they call them: indeed, on the contrary, I have proposed to myself
hitherto that a perfect knowledge of that part of Arithmetic depends on
