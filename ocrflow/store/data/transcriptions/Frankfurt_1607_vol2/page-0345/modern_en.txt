THEREFORE, to return to the plan, let us begin Euclid’s [work] itself,
in his usual manner, from explanations of the terms which must be used;
he declares in the first place what magnitudes are said to be commensurable,
defining them to be those which the same measure measures.  A
As two magnitudes A, B, which the same mea-                 B
sure C measures, (for C measures A, repeated four times,
and taken three times measures B,) are said to be commensurable. In the
same way, [they] are commensurable if [they are] a [line] of 20 palms and a
line of 13 palms: because that line measures as well five palms, as half a palm,
as a third part of one palm, etc. Similarly, surfaces are said to be commensurable
which one and the same surface measures; likewise bodies, commensurable solids,
which the same body or solid measures.
