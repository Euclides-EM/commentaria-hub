EUCLID’S
ELEMENTS
TENTH.

DEFINITIONS.
I.

COMMENSURABLE magnitudes are said to be,
which the same measure measures.

Euclid has completed in the preceding three books
those things which pertain to numbers, as much as seemed
sufficient for understanding geometrical matters: Now in this
tenth book he undertakes the discussion of lines
commensurable and incommensurable, for which
cause we said above that the treatment of numbers was undertaken
by him. For without knowledge of these lines
many magnitudes, both solid and plane, can neither be perfectly
understood, nor, when occasion requires, be brought into work and use;
because for the most part their sides are incommensurable: which can also
be said of the planes themselves and the solids, since these too
often turn out to be incommensurable in number, as we shall demonstrate
at the end of this book. But since this book is beset with many
difficulties, because of the obscurity of the lines of which it treats,
I shall strain every nerve of my diligence in it, so that from those things
which up to now have been demonstrated by Euclid,
it may be made so plain and easy, that without much labor
by anyone who has rightly understood the demonstrations of the preceding
books, it can be perceived: For I cannot go along with the opinion of those
who think that for its understanding that part of Arithmetic is necessary
which sets forth discourse concerning the roots of numbers,
both rational and irrational, as they call them: Indeed, on the contrary,
I am thoroughly persuaded that the perfect knowledge
of that part of Arithmetic depends on this 10th book; so far
