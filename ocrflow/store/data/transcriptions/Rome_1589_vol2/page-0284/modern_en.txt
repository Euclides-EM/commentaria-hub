  THEREFORE, that we may return to what was undertaken, Euclid, beginning
the matter itself, in his manner, from explanations of the terms
which will have to be used; declares in the first place what magnitudes
are called commensurable, defining them to be those which the same
measure measures. As two magnitudes
A, B, which the same measure C
measures (for C measures A, taken
four times, and B, taken three times) are called commensurable.
In the same way commensurable are a line of 20 palms
and a line of 13 palms; because a line of as much as one palm, as
of half a palm, as of a third part of one palm, etc., measures them.
Similarly commensurable are said to be surfaces which one
and the same surface measures; likewise bodies, that is, solids commensurable,
which the same body, or solid, measures.

II.

INCOMMENSURABLE, however,
are those for which it happens that no common
measure can be found.

SUCH magnitudes are the diameter of any
square, and its side; since they have no common
measure, as will be demonstrated by Euclid in the last proposition
of this book. There are also very many other incommensurable
lines, to which, namely, no common measure can in any way
be given, many of which this book explains, and teaches
by what method they can be found. Again, surfaces will be called
incommensurable, and solids incommensurable, which admit
no common measure—.
