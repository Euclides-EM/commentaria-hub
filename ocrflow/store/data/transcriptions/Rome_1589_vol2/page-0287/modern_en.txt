IF some straight line of known magnitude be proposed, to which
of all other [lines], which are compared with it, some are
commensurable in length and in power, but some in
power only: likewise some are incommensurable with it in
length only; but some in length and in power, as in this
tenth book will be demonstrated in various places. For neither
does Euclid, from what has been said, show, or gather,
(although his words seem to sound thus) that these things are so:
but he only indicates to us, and in some manner hints, that
some lines can be said to be commensurable with the proposed
straight line in length and in power,
but some in power only, &c: which indeed in no way follows
from what has been said, but will be demonstrated in what follows,
as we have said.

BUT that proposed line, by reason of which others are
commensurable or incommensurable, is called in Greek
ῥητὴ; but in Latin Rational; because it is always set down as
known, and known, but others, when compared with it, are not always
known, although each separately always exists as a certain magnitude
and known; since it can be divided into any number of equal parts.

VI.

AND those commensurable with this either in
length and in power, or in power
only, [are] Rational.

HE TEACHES that other lines, commensurable with that which is called
Rational in whatever way, are also called Rational;
not indeed from position, as that [one], but because when compared with it
they are found commensurable with it either in length and in power,
or in power only—.

THEREFORE according to the opinion of Euclid, the square root of
this number 20. or 1000. &c. Or what is the same, the straight line
whose square is 20. or 1000. &c. is called Rational;
