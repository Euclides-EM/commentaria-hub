  But that proposed line, by reference to which others are
commensurable or incommensurable, is called by the Greeks
ῥητὴ, and by the Latins Rational; since it is always set down
as certain and known, whereas others, compared with it, are not always
known, although each, taken separately, exists in some way
as certain and known; and any one can be divided into equal parts in any way.

VI.

And those commensurable with it either in length
and power, or in power only, [are] Rational.

He teaches that other lines, commensurable with that which is called Rational
in whatever way, are also called Rational: not indeed
by position, as that one, but because when compared with it they are found
commensurable with it in length and power, or in power only.
  Thus, according to the meaning of Euclid, the square root of this
number 20, or 100, etc. Or what is the same, the straight line whose
square is 20, or 100, etc. is called Rational, since
in power it is commensurable with the Rational line, although in length
it is incommensurable with it. Therefore not a few arithmeticians are deceived,
who call it Irrational, because it cannot
be expressed by a number.

VII.

But those incommensurable with this, let them be called
Irrational.

Since in the preceding definition Euclid called those lines
which are commensurable in length and power, or which are commensurable
in power only, with the proposed Rational straight line, Rational;

it is clear that here he calls those Irrational which are incommensurable
with the proposed Rational straight line in all
ways, namely in length and power, but not in length only.
From which it is also evident that the square root of this
number 20, or 1000, etc. or (what is the same) the
line whose square is 20, or 1000, etc. cannot be called
Irrational, since it is not incommensurable in length and power, but in
length only, with the proposed Rational line.

VIII.

And the square which is made from the proposed
straight line, let it be called Rational.

Just as that line which is set down as certain and
known is called Rational: so also the square described on it
is called Rational, because it too is certain and known
by comparison; other surfaces, compared with it, are called commensurable
or incommensurable.

IX.

And those commensurable with it indeed, [are] Ra-
tional.

All plane surfaces commensurable with the square of the proposed Rational
line are called Rational; not indeed
by position, as that one, but because when compared with it they are found
either commensurable with it, or incommensurable: just as
also lines which are commensurable with the Rational line in whatever way
are called Rational.
  But since, if the powers of lines are commensurable with the
square of the proposed Rational line, they are at least
commensurable in power with the Rational line, from the 3rd
definition it is clear that these too should be called
according to definition 6, Ra-
tional.
