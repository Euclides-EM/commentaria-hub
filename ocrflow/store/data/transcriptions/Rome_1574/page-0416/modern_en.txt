Euclid in the preceding
three books has set forth those things which pertain to numbers,
as much as seemed sufficient for understanding geometrical matters:
Now in this tenth book we proceed to the discussion
of commensurable and in-
commensurable lines, for whose sake we said above
that he undertook the treatment of numbers.
For without knowledge of these lines many
magnitudes, both solid and plane, can neither be perfectly understood,
nor, when occasion requires, be applied to work and use; because
for the most part their sides are incommensurable: which can also be said
of the planes themselves and solids, since these too
often exist incommensurable in number, as we shall demonstrate
at the end of this book. But since this book is obstructed by many
difficulties, because of the obscurity of the lines of which it treats;
I shall strain every nerve of my industry in it, so that from those things
which up to now have been demonstrated by Euclid,
it may be made so plain and easy that without great labor
it can be grasped by anyone who nevertheless has understood
the demonstrations of the preceding books. Nor indeed can I agree with the opinion
