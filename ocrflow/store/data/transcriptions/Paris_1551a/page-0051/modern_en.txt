EUCLID’S ELEMENTS.

[He] would separate things which by their own nature are joined, and [make them] contraries. Which almost all mathematicians have been seen to observe most scrupulously: as in this place, because lines commensurable in length are also commensurable in power, when he was speaking of those commensurable in length, he wished to add “and in power.” But when [speaking] of those commensurable in power, he added “only.” For lines which are commensurable in power are not straightway also [commensurable] in length. But these ῥηταί, that is, rational [lines], which in this place have received such a designation, are now not ῥηταί by supposition, which was that former ῥητή; but they are such because of the relation which they have to that [one]. Because they are either in length and in power together, or in power only, commensurable with that ῥητή which is so called in the first place. Moreover here it must be noted that those expressions, “in length and in power,” or “in power only,” are joined with those expressions “commensurable” or “incommensurable”; but with those expressions “rational” or “irrational” they are never added, so that lines are said to be rational or irrational in length or in power. Which Campanus seems to have used indiscriminately. If you desire more, I restrain here the eager mind of learning, lest you abandon the footsteps of the author, and of your same guide, who indeed thought the principles should be handed down most simply, so that he might inform the minds of learners only by the bare notion of the thing. Which is both truer, and more suited for teaching.

But those lines which are incommensurable with that ῥητή, that is, with the rational [line] in the first place, are called ἄλογοι, that is, irrational.
