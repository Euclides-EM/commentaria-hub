since especially commensurable magnitudes have that
proportion among themselves which number has to number.

Campanus, a man who from studies of geometry has deservedly
obtained praise, rightly inscribes that principle among the other
principles of the seventh book, although the Greek exemplar has
nothing of the sort. A number is said to number another, which,
multiplied by some [number], produces it. Of which
let this be an example. Three numbers three
when multiplied by one. The same three numbers six
when multiplied by two; numbers nine
when multiplied by three; numbers 12 by 4;
numbers 9; the others infinitely many. Yet that same
three by no means numbers other numbers interposed between the higher ones.
For it neither numbers unity itself, nor two.
For the greater can in no way number the lesser:
just as in magnitudes (as we said before) a greater
measure does not measure a lesser magnitude by itself.
Nor indeed does three number 4 or 5. For multiplied by one
it makes nothing more than 3; but multiplied by two,
it exceeds both 4 and 5; much more
if it shall have been multiplied by another three or by some greater number,
it will have exceeded the same 4 and 5. The same reasoning holds for
seven, eight, ten, and eleven, if you try to number any one of
