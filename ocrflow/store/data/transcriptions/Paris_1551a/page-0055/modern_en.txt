One thing is what I think should be noticed in this place. When
    he had said that ἄλογα are those which are incommensurable with
    themselves, he wished to add about the lines describing those
    incommensurable [squares], and he likewise called them ἄλογες: although
    earlier, when he was speaking about things commensurable with
    themselves, he said nothing about the lines describing those
    commensurable [squares]. But he omitted this for this reason, because
    he seemed to himself to have done enough, and to have hinted at these,
    when he was speaking about lines commensurable with themselves, under
    those words, “or in power” only. For the power of a line is its square,
    as we said before. But if the square is commensurable with itself,
    the line also which makes that square will be commensurable at least
    in power. And so, by the definition of lines commensurable with
    themselves, it will also be [so].
    There exists a little book under the name of Aristotle, περὶ ἀτόμων γραμμῶν,
    which indeed is understood easily, from many places of Aristotle himself,
    not to be Aristotle’s: yet its learning makes it so that it ought to be
    attributed to someone from that noble and learned family of the
    Peripatetics. In it you will read many things conducive to the
    understanding of these principles. Of which we will note this one thing,
    which can seem to contain almost the sum of the whole explanation of the
    matter: that the commensurability and incommensurability of magnitudes
