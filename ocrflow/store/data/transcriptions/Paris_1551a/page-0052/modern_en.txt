Understand that statement about lines incommensurable in
length and in power. For there cannot be any lines incom-
mensurable in power only, such that the same lines are not
also incommensurable in length. Nor should you think that
Euclid is here treating of lines incommensurable in length
only, but commensurable in power. For he has lately placed
these among the ῥητὰς, that is, the rational. But here he
wishes lines incommensurable in every respect, that is, in
power and in length, to be called ἄλογος. And since this was
not so understood by Campanus, he seems to have brought
upon himself the cause of the error, which he also handed
down to posterity, as we shall soon say. What ῥητὸν and
ἄῤῥητον are has been said before. But as to this word ἄλογοι,
it may be learned from the commentaries of Proclus that
ῥητὸν and ἀῤῥητον are opposed by privation, so that ἀῤῥητον
is contrary to τῷ ῥητῷ. But ἄλογον is more often used in
place of this word ἀῤῥητον, although ἄλογοι and ἀῤῥητον are
the same. The one is from ἀπὸ τῆς ἀριθμῷ, the other from
ἀπὸ τῆς λέξεως, which signify the same. Therefore an ἄλογος
line will be one whose ratio, its length compared to the
length of the ῥητῆς, that is, of the line itself in the first place,
and by supposition rational, cannot be expressed by any
numbers. Nor should you think that ἄλογοι are so called by
privation of τῆς λόγου, that is, of proportion. For there is a
certain proportion of alogous lines among themselves, not
indeed wholly unknown to us, much less escaping the
knowledge of nature, as some wish; otherwise Euclid would
have undertaken that work in vain, indeed in a book in which
he does nothing else than teach the affections of those lines,
and the proportions which those ἄλογοι lines hold among
themselves. But for this reason they are called ἄλογοι,
