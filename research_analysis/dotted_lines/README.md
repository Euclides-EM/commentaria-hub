# Dotted Lines

## Central Conclusion

Early modern printed mathematics used dotted marks in two related but distinct regimes. In arithmetic, dots could represent countable units and stage number as a discrete multitude. In geometry, dotted lines remained continuous lines but marked their diagrammatic status: auxiliary, hypothetical or impossible, hidden or projected, operative, explanatory, transferred, or visually secondary.

The broader corpus shows that geometrical dotted lines were especially useful in practical geometry, instruments, architecture, perspective, surveying, and related technical fields. These books used them for construction operations, compass traces, hidden parts, plan/elevation relations, area transfer, and layer management. In editions of Euclid, comparable graphic distinctions were redirected toward proof, contradiction, scholia, commentary, praxis, and complex demonstrations. The evidence supports a shared visual ecology, not a claim of direct influence.

## Functional Taxonomy

Retained function labels should distinguish:

1. counterfactual or impossible lines;
2. auxiliary demonstrative lines;
3. operative construction lines or compass traces;
4. hidden, projected, interior, or spatial lines;
5. explanatory overlays and diagram-layer management;
6. transfer, decomposition, or recomposition boundaries;
7. token, counting-board, ruler-like, or arithmetic dots;
8. material design lines intended for a physical object.

Cross-cutting checks include whether the line is mentioned in the text, belongs to a main demonstration/praxis/scholium, is compass- or ruler-generated, and functions as reader guidance.

## Interpretive Guardrails

- Do not collapse arithmetic tokens, segmented rulers, and geometrical dotted lines.
- Do not infer direct transmission from chronology alone.
- Reprints may establish persistence but should not inflate the count of independent innovations.
- Format metadata is too incomplete for a strong format claim.
- Strong historical examples still require checking against the source image and accompanying text.
